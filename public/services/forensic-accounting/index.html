Start here

What should you do first if you suspect fraud?

What you do in the first few days can decide what can be proved later. General steps only. Your attorney should guide anything legal.

  1. Do not confront anyone yet

    A warning gives a person time to destroy records or explain things away.

  2. Keep the records as they are

    Do not delete, edit or tidy anything. Make copies of bank statements, checks and system reports.

  3. Limit who knows

    Tell only the people who must know, and talk where the person you suspect cannot overhear.

  4. Call your attorney

    Especially if law enforcement, an insurance claim or a lawsuit may follow.

  5. Read your insurance policy

    Some business policies cover employee theft and set deadlines for telling the insurer.

What it looks like

What does fraud usually look like in a small business?

Fraud in an owner-run business often comes from inside and hides in ordinary transactions.

Fake or padded vendors

Invoices from a company that does not exist, or a real vendor billing for work never done.

Duplicate payments

The same invoice paid twice, with the second payment sent somewhere else.

Payroll schemes

A former employee still on the payroll, extra hours that were never worked or changed pay rates.

Cash skimming

Customer payments taken before they are ever recorded in the books.

Card and expense abuse

Personal purchases on company cards or expense reports with altered receipts.

Check and transfer changes

Altered payees, forged signatures or transfers to accounts the business does not own.

Profit and loss analysis

What if profit dropped but nothing looks stolen?

A profit and loss analysis takes your income statement apart to find where the money went. Ray compares periods, jobs, products, customers and cost lines until the drop is explained.

The answer is sometimes fraud. More often it is prices that did not keep up with costs, a few jobs or customers that lose money, waste, or errors in how costs are recorded. When records are missing or incomplete, Ray can rebuild the numbers from bank statements and other source documents.

What Ray compares

Where a profit drop usually hides

  • Gross margin by job, product or customer
  • Price changes against cost changes
  • Labor hours against output
  • Overhead growth against sales growth
  • Entries that were reclassified, reversed or posted late

  2. Gather the records

Know the limits

What can a forensic review tell you?

What it can show

The facts in the records

Which transactions happened, when, for how much, who approved them and where the money went. That gives you and your attorney a clear basis for your next decision.

What stays with you

Decisions about people

Ray is an accountant. Whether someone broke the law, and what to do about it, is for you, your attorney and, if needed, law enforcement.

Can Ray find out whether an employee is stealing?

Ray can examine the records for the signs that fraud leaves behind, such as duplicate payments, unknown vendors and altered checks, and report what he finds. No review can promise to find everything, and the decision about what to do next stays with you and your attorney.

How long does a forensic review take?

It depends on how many years and accounts are involved and how complete the records are. Ray scopes the work with you first, and a large review can be done in stages so you see findings as they come.

Will the person I suspect find out?

Ray can work with a small group of people you choose and keep the work out of sight of the rest of the business. Tell him on the first call who must not know.

Can the findings be used in court or for an insurance claim?

If a matter may go to court or to an insurer, bring your attorney in early. They can decide how the work should be set up and documented for that use.

What records does Ray need?

Usually bank and credit card statements, the general ledger, the vendor list, payroll reports and copies of checks. Ray will tell you exactly what he needs for your question.
